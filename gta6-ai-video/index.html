Why game-style clips are everywhere, and what they actually are

Game-style AI clips are everywhere for a reason that has nothing to do with the game: the visual grammar is already familiar. Third-person framing, a moving camera behind a character, saturated dusk light and a city that looks expensive — viewers recognise the genre in half a second, so a fifteen-second clip does not need to explain itself.

Why the look is easy to imitate

Game trailers solved a hard problem years ago: how to show a world without a plot. They use a small set of repeated moves — the trailing camera, the slow orbit around a character, the cut to a wide establishing shot, the low-angle hero frame. Those moves are cheap to prompt and forgiving of model artefacts, because the viewer's attention is on the motion and the environment rather than on a face.

What actually breaks

Faces in close-up, because game characters sit in an uncanny valley that a model makes worse. Text on signage, which turns to glyph soup. Crowds, where limbs merge. And long continuous takes, because the environment starts to reassemble itself in the background while the camera moves. Keep cuts short and keep the camera moving; both hide the seams.

Prompting the genre

Name the camera behaviour before the content: "camera trails behind a figure walking through a rain-slick night market". Then light: neon practicals, wet reflections. Then the constraint: no close-up, no readable text. Aspect ratio matters more than usual here — the genre is widescreen, and a vertical crop destroys the establishing shots that make it read as a game rather than a phone video.

The rights question

Style is not the same as assets. A clip that evokes a genre is one thing; a clip that reproduces a specific character, logo or map is another, and the second is where fan projects get takedowns. If the output is for a brand, keep the reference generic — "third-person open-world game" rather than a named title — and have the client sign off on the brief before generation, not after.

Where it is genuinely useful

Concept trailers for games that do not exist yet, mood boards for a pitch, and social clips that ride a trend without claiming to be official. It is a pre-visualisation tool that happens to be publishable, which is a narrower claim than "AI makes games" and a much more defensible one.
