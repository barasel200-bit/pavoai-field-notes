Korean-language prompting is not English prompting with the words swapped. Word order, honorifics and compound nouns all change how a model parses a sentence, and video models are sensitive to that in ways that are easy to mistake for "the model is bad at Korean". It usually is not. The prompt is bad at Korean.

Why the language matters more than it should

Most video models are trained predominantly on English captions. A Korean prompt therefore travels through a thinner part of the model's knowledge, and small ambiguities get resolved differently. The classic failure: a sentence that is perfectly polite and grammatically complete produces a static, over-composed shot, because the model reads the honorific register as formality rather than as an instruction about motion.

What we compared

Five matched prompt pairs, Korean and English, same subject, same camera instruction, same aspect ratio. The pattern that held across the set: Korean prompts needed the action verb earlier in the sentence and the camera instruction as a separate clause, or the model dropped one of the two. Once the prompts were restructured that way, output quality converged with the English versions. The language was never the ceiling; the sentence shape was.

Prompt patterns that survive translation

Put the subject first, then one concrete action, then a camera clause, then light. Avoid stacking two actions in one sentence; Korean allows it elegantly and the model does not. Keep honorifics out of the instruction clause entirely — write the direction in plain form, and the on-screen text, if any, in the register you actually want. If a specific object matters, name it with the English loanword as well as the Korean term, because the model's Korean vocabulary is thinner for technical nouns.

Localisation workflow

Write the shot in Korean, then have someone who does not know the intended output translate it back to English cold. Where the back-translation drifts, the model will drift too. Fix those clauses before spending credits. Keep a glossary of product names and brand terms that must not be translated, and paste it into every prompt — consistency across a campaign is worth more than any single good clip.

Caveats

This is field observation, not a benchmark. Model versions change monthly and the balance between Korean and English prompting shifts with them. Re-test the five-pair comparison whenever you upgrade, and keep the pairs in version control so the comparison is actually comparable. The same discipline applies to Korean text-to-video more generally.
